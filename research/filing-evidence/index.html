Show Your Work: Can Small Open Models Read SEC Filings with Evidence a Machine Can Check?

Filing-Evidence: a pre-registered benchmark of ten open models on one consumer GPU

Pramana Research17 November 2026Version 1.0
Download PDF Code and protocol Dataset and every answer Results per model

In brief

We asked ten openly available AI models, small enough to run on one gaming graphics card, to find 1,000 numbers in recent SEC filings. Every answer had to come with the page and the exact line it was read from, so a computer could check it, or to say "not found" when the number wasn't there.

  1. Small open models can do much of this job, and the gap between them is large. In the plain setup accuracy ranges from 17.1% to 82.7%. Qwen3.5-9B is ahead of every other model by a statistically clear margin.
  2. Demanding a checkable quote works, but it isn't enough on its own. The checker stops most wrong answers. Yet the answers that pass every check include 2.4–31.8% wrong ones, depending on model and setup. Those are the dangerous errors: correct-looking evidence, wrong number.
  3. Those errors are specific and fixable. They are almost all units read wrongly or the neighbouring column (another year or quarter) of the right line, not invented numbers.
  4. Letting the model think helps more than letting it retry. Thinking cut wrong-but-passing answers for every model that has the mode, at 8–67 times the generation cost. Telling a model which check failed and letting it retry adds 0.1–3.9 points of accuracy but never reduces the dangerous errors, and for 4 models it increases them: told that its quote failed, a model often finds a line that contains its wrong number.

Abstract

Language models are increasingly used to read financial documents, but a fluent answer is not a verified one. We ask whether small open models, the kind that run on a single consumer GPU, can extract numbers from SEC filings with evidence a machine can check: the page and the verbatim line the number was read from. We built Filing-Evidence, 1,000 questions over 90 recent 10-K and 10-Q filings of 45 US companies, with gold answers taken from the filers' own inline XBRL tags, in eight kinds of content including segment tables, numbers in prose and questions whose answer is not on the pages shown. The protocol, metrics and statistics were registered before any model ran. We evaluated ten instruct models from ten companies (7–21B parameters) on one RTX 4090 in three setups: plain, check-and-retry, and the model's own thinking mode. Accuracy with checkable evidence ranges from 17.1% to 82.7% in the plain setup; the best result is 84.5% (Qwen3.5-9B). An automatic checker rejects most wrong answers, yet 16 to 167 answers per 1,000 pass every check and are still wrong; across the ten models 57% of these are scale errors and 30% the wrong column of the right line. Thinking reduces such errors significantly for 4 of 5 models; retrying after a failed check raises accuracy by up to 3.9 points but never reduces them. We release the questions, every model answer, the code and the protocol.

1 · Introduction

An analyst who copies a number from a filing can show where it came from. A language model that does the same job usually can't, or it gives a citation nobody checks. For a model to be trusted with this work its answers have to be auditable: each number should come with evidence that can be verified quickly, preferably by software.

The question is simple to state. If every answer must name the page and quote the exact line containing the number, how often are small open models right, how often are they wrong in a way the quote doesn't reveal, and do two cheap, training-free interventions help: checking the answer automatically and letting the model try again, or switching on the model's own reasoning mode? We ask it of models that fit on one 24 GB consumer GPU, because those are the models an organisation can run on its own hardware, with its own documents, at low cost.

We registered three research questions before running any model (the full protocol is in the repository):

  • RQ1. How reliably do ~10B-parameter open instruct models extract a requested number from an SEC filing with evidence that can be checked automatically, and answer "not found" when the number isn't on the pages provided?
  • RQ2. Where do they fail: in which kinds of filing content, and with which kinds of error?
  • RQ3. How much do (a) automatic checking with one retry and (b) the model's own thinking mode help, and at what cost in tokens and time?

Our working thesis was that a small open model on one consumer GPU can be a trustworthy reader of SEC filings if every answer must carry evidence a machine can check. The results support the first half of that sentence more than the second: requiring checkable evidence makes most errors visible, but a specific class of errors passes through it. Section 7 discusses what closes that gap.

2 · Benchmark design

2.1 Filings and answer key

We took the most recent 10-K and 10-Q filed on or before 15 November 2026 by 45 large US companies (90 filings): forty in nine sector groups chosen to cover different statement layouts (technology, banks, other financials, insurers, REITs, utilities, healthcare, consumer, industrials and others), plus five added for segment coverage. Banks, insurers, REITs and utilities are included deliberately, because their statements use line items and layouts unlike an industrial company's.

Gold answers come from the filings' own inline XBRL tags: the machine-readable layer that filers embed in the document to label each number with its concept, period, unit, scale and dimensions. We use only tags whose number is displayed on a page, and record the page and the line where it is shown. The answer key is therefore the filer's own statement, not our annotation.

2.2 Questions

Each question names an item, a company and a period in words ("What was Apple Inc.'s total liabilities as of June 27, 2026?") and comes with three pages of the filing: the page that shows the answer, an adjacent page and a random distractor, in random order. Questions fall into eight slices of 125 each:

S8 asks for something that is not on the pages provided: a period not shown, an item the company doesn't report, a quarter asked of an annual report, or an item that is elsewhere in the filing but not on these pages. The correct answer is "not found". Strict rules decide which facts can become questions: the number must be a column figure on a row with a printed label, the row must be fully populated, the period's year must be visible on the page, and the question names the item by its printed row label when that label is unique on the shown pages.

2.3 Quality control and freeze

Before any model ran, 100 questions (stratified across slices) were checked by hand against the rendered filing in each round; the protocol required at least 98 correct. Rounds one to three failed on wording (87, 76 and 96 of 100), never on gold values: all 400 hand-checked gold values across the four rounds were right. Round four passed with 98 of 100, and the dataset was frozen with a SHA-256 fingerprint (4fa8f252292167fb…). Every result cites it, and the published data verifies against it.

2.4 The checker

Models answer in a fixed JSON format: the value, its scale ("in millions"), the page number and the line quoted from the page, or {"status": "not_found"}. The checker then applies four automatic tests (Figure 2) and compares the value with the answer key. The sign is not graded, because filers display negative numbers in inconsistent ways; values are compared after scale, and per-share amounts and percentages as displayed.

Every answer receives exactly one outcome. Accuracy is the share of the 875 answerable questions (S1–S7) answered correctly with evidence that passes the checks: a right number with an unusable quote does not count. Confident-wrong rate is the share of all 1,000 answers that pass every check but are wrong, and is the metric that matters most for a system that would act on answers the checker accepts. Abstention accuracy is the share of S8 questions answered "not found". Every rate carries a 95% Wilson interval; differences are tested with paired exact McNemar tests on the same questions and Holm-corrected within each family of comparisons.

3 · Models and setups

Ten general instruct models, each from a different company, all served with vLLM 0.33.0 on one NVIDIA RTX 4090 (24 GB) at temperature 0, with each model's own chat template and its exact Hugging Face revision pinned. Models are used as published: no fine-tuning, no finance-specific models, no prompt tuning per model; the prompt is identical for every model. Qwen3-8B, the model of the pilot study that motivated the benchmark, was run as a supplementary baseline and is reported separately.

4 · RQ1: How reliable are they?

Accuracy in the plain setup spans a wide range, from 17.1% for OLMo-3-7B to 82.7% (80.1–85.1%) for Qwen3.5-9B. The paired tests separate the ten models into clear tiers (Figure 6): Qwen3.5-9B is significantly more accurate than every other model; gpt-oss-20b, Granite-4.2-8B and GLM-4.6V-Flash cannot be told apart from each other; then come Ministral-3-8B, a group of Gemma-3-12B, Llama-3.1-8B and Phi-4, and finally Nemotron-Nano-9B-v2 and OLMo-3-7B. The best single result is 84.5% (Qwen3.5-9B, check + retry).

The checker is the reason these accuracy figures mean something. An answer only counts when its quote is on the cited page and contains the number. Without that requirement the figures would be higher for every model: a right number with an unusable quote is common for weaker models (Figure 7). For a reader of the output that difference is the whole point: a right number you can't verify is not much more useful than a wrong one.

"Not found" is handled well by most models. Abstention accuracy in the plain setup ranges from 77.6% to 100.0%: models rarely invent a number for something that isn't on the page. Most invented answers come from one kind of trap: a quarterly figure asked of an annual report, which the filing can't contain. Gemma-3-12B, the weakest abstainer among the ten (77.6%), answered 17 of those 31 correctly, and the supplementary Qwen3-8B only 6.

5 · RQ2: Where do they fail?

5.1 By kind of content

The main statements are the easiest content: pooled over the ten models, 79.2% of current-period questions are answered correctly. Accuracy falls on prior-period columns (67.1%), in notes (67.4%), and most of all for segments (46.0%), numbers in prose (41.4%) and units and per-share figures (50.5%). Even Qwen3.5-9B, strong everywhere else, answers only 57.6% of prose questions correctly. These are the places where a filing's layout stops doing the work: a segment table's columns are business lines rather than years, and a number in a sentence has no column header at all.

5.2 What the dangerous errors are

Across the ten models there are 944 confident-wrong answers in the plain setup. Only 13% are a wrong item. The rest are near misses on the right line:

  • Scale or unit (57%). The model quotes the correct line and gives the correct digits, but states the wrong scale, most often "none" for a table headed "(In millions)", or reads a share count as shares rather than millions of shares. The checker can't catch this, because the quote is genuine and contains the number; the units are stated once, at the top of the table.
  • Wrong period or column (30%). The model quotes the right line and takes the figure for another year, quarter or year-to-date column. The quote again contains the number, so it passes.

Scale errors are concentrated in a few models: they are 116 of Phi-4's 151 confident-wrong answers, but 2 of Qwen3.5-9B's 36. The secondary metric accuracy ignoring scale shows how much of each gap is units rather than finding the number: for Phi-4 it is 70.5% against an accuracy of 57.3%.

6 · RQ3: Retry or think?

6.1 Check + retry

When an answer fails a check, the model is told which one ("the quoted line does not appear on page 54") and answers once more; it is never told the answer. This raises accuracy by 0.1 to 3.9 points, significantly for 8 of the ten models, at little cost: only answers that fail a check are asked again (5–54% of answers, depending on the model).

But retrying never reduces confident-wrong answers, and for 4 models (Ministral-3-8B, Phi-4, Gemma-3-12B, Llama-3.1-8B) it significantly increases them. The mechanism is visible in the answers: told that its quote failed, a model often keeps its wrong number and finds a line that contains it, which now passes. For Phi-4, 16 of 177 retried answers moved from "caught" to "confident-wrong" in this way. A retry teaches the model to satisfy the checker, not to be right. Nemotron-Nano-9B-v2 shows the opposite failure: asked to retry, it answered "not found" almost every time.

6.2 Thinking

Five of the ten models have a reasoning mode (as does the supplementary Qwen3-8B). Thinking improved accuracy for 4 of them, by up to 11.4 points (GLM-4.6V-Flash), and reduced confident-wrong answers for all 5: Qwen3.5-9B 36→16, Granite-4.2-8B 64→38, Nemotron-Nano-9B-v2 105→72, gpt-oss-20b 112→56, GLM-4.6V-Flash 110→61 answers per 1,000 (4 of these reductions are significant). The lowest confident-wrong rate in the study is Qwen3.5-9B with thinking: 16 in 1,000.

The exception is Qwen3.5-9B, the strongest plain model, whose accuracy fell from 82.7% to 74.6% with thinking. The cause is the token budget, not the reasoning: it reasoned at length (mean 3,574 tokens) and hit the pre-registered 8,192-token limit on 180 questions, leaving 88 with no parseable answer. On the questions it finished, its accuracy was 85.1%.

Thinking is expensive. It multiplies generated tokens by 67× (Qwen3.5-9B), 52× (Granite-4.2-8B), 12× (Nemotron-Nano-9B-v2), 18× (gpt-oss-20b), 8× (GLM-4.6V-Flash). On one RTX 4090 with 16 concurrent requests, a full 1,000-question run took 14 minutes for GLM-4.6V-Flash with thinking and 182 for Granite-4.2-8B, against 3–7 minutes in the plain setup.

7 · Discussion

Checkable evidence is necessary, not sufficient. Requiring a verbatim quote turned most errors into visible, rejectable ones: the checker is cheap, needs no model and no gold answer, and could run in any system that uses these models. What it cannot do is check that the number is the right figure on the line, or that its units are right. The residue is small for the best configurations (1.6% of answers at best) and substantial for most (up to 16.7%).

The residue is structured. 87% of confident-wrong answers are the right line read wrongly: the units header misapplied, or the neighbouring column taken. Both are checkable in principle. A stronger checker could require the model to quote the column header and the units statement as well as the line, and verify that the chosen figure sits under the quoted header; a units check could compare the stated scale with the table's own heading. These are engineering steps, not research problems, and they are the natural next version of the benchmark.

Retrying is not a free win. Feeding a model the name of the failed check improves accuracy a little, but it also turns some caught errors into uncaught ones. A system that retries should treat a passed retry with more suspicion than a passed first answer, or re-verify it more strictly.

Model choice matters more than setup. The spread between the best and worst model in the plain setup is 66 points; the largest gain from any setup is about 11. And the strongest model in the plain setup, Qwen3.5-9B, reaches 84.5% with no reasoning at all.

8 · Limitations

  • One prompt, temperature 0, one run. Results are for a single fixed prompt shared by all models; a prompt tuned per model could change rankings. Each configuration was run once.
  • US large-cap filings, text only. 45 companies, recent 10-K and 10-Q filings. No small caps, foreign filers, scanned documents, charts or images.
  • Three pages per question. The model is shown the right page plus two others; finding the page in a whole filing is a harder, separate task.
  • Units stated off-page. After the results we found 24 answerable questions (2.7%) whose page shows no units statement, because the filer states it once on an earlier page. The frozen dataset was not changed. Excluding them moves no model's accuracy by more than 1.8 points and changes no ranking (Appendix A).
  • Error categories were not reviewed by hand. The protocol planned a hand review of 20 answers per model to confirm the automatic error categories; it was not carried out (recorded as a deviation). Outcomes themselves are exact, by construction; the categories are rule-based.
  • Thinking budget. The 8,192-token reasoning limit was fixed in advance; it truncated 180 of Qwen3.5-9B's and 97 of gpt-oss-20b's answers. A larger budget would likely raise their thinking-mode results.
  • Contamination. Models may have seen older filings in training. 89 of the 90 filings were filed in 2026, and eight of the ten models were released before 2026. A memorised answer must still come with a verbatim quote from the pages shown.
  • Precision. Phi-4 and Gemma-3-12B were run with FP8 weights to fit in 24 GB, and gpt-oss-20b in its native MXFP4; results at full precision may differ slightly.

9 · Reproducibility

Everything needed to check or repeat this study is public. The code, the protocol (with its deviations log) and each run's summary are on GitHub; the questions, page texts, every model reply including its reasoning, and the server logs are on Hugging Face. Each run records the model revision, the exact vLLM command, the prompt hash and the dataset fingerprint.

git clone https://github.com/pramana-research/filing-evidence.git && cd filing-evidence
uv sync --all-extras
uv run python hf_data.py pull                       # data, verified against the frozen fingerprint
uv run python runner.py qwen3.5-9b C1 --regrade     # re-grade stored replies (no GPU)
bash run_all.sh qwen3.5-9b                          # rerun a model (one 24 GB GPU)

Re-grading every stored reply reproduces every number on this page. These pages are generated from the stored results by report/build.py in the same repository.

D · Deviations from the protocol

All changes made after the protocol was registered are logged with their date and reason in PROTOCOL.md §12. The ones that affect how results are read: the served context is 16,384 tokens (8,192 would have truncated thinking); C2 reuses C1's first answer; Qwen3-8B is a supplementary baseline; gpt-oss-20b's plain setup uses its lowest reasoning effort; a secondary accuracy-ignoring-scale metric was added; the error taxonomy counts a misplaced decimal point as a scale error; and the planned hand review of error categories was not carried out. All except the last were made before any main result.
